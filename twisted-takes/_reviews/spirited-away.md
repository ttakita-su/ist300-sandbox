## Premise

Ten-year-old Chihiro is sulking in the back seat when her parents take a wrong turn into what looks like an abandoned theme park. By nightfall it's crowded with spirits, her parents are in serious trouble, and she must find work at a bathhouse for the gods.

## The Take

Most fantasy heroes get a sword. Chihiro gets a scrub brush. Hayao Miyazaki builds the film around labor, manners and small acts of nerve, which is why it feels so unlike Western quest stories. She grows up by showing up for shifts and keeping her word.

## What Works

The bathhouse is drawn like a real workplace, with a boiler room, steam and shared staff sleeping quarters, so the magic feels grounded. Joe Hisaishi's gentle, piano-led themes leave room for quiet, and a nearly wordless train ride lands harder than any chase. Chihiro's slouchy, whiny body language early on is unusually honest for an animated kid.

## What May Divide Viewers

Everything runs on dream logic. Rules appear unexplained, side characters drift in and out, and some imagery turns grotesque. If you need every mechanic spelled out, you may feel adrift.

## Who Should Watch

Animation skeptics, people who like getting lost in a place, and braver grade-schoolers watching with someone older.
