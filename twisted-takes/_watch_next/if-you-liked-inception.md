[Inception]({{ '/reviews/inception/' | relative_url }}) is technically a heist movie, but the heist is rarely what people come back for. If it stuck with you, you're probably chasing a few things at once: rules you pick up on the fly, a story that trusts you to keep up, big practical spectacle, and a personal ache sitting under all the clever machinery.

No single movie hits every one of those. So instead of three copies, here are three films that each pull on a different thread. Pick the one that matches the part you liked most.

## Interstellar: for the emotional scale

[Interstellar]({{ '/reviews/interstellar/' | relative_url }}) comes from the same director, Christopher Nolan, and you'll recognize the instincts: enormous sets, a booming Hans Zimmer score, and a plot that treats time as something that can stretch. The difference is where the pressure comes from. In Inception, time is a puzzle piece. Here, it's a cost. Every delay means missing years with the people back home, and the movie never lets you forget it.

It's also far more openly sentimental. A father and daughter sit at the center, and the film goes big on feeling in a way Inception mostly keeps buttoned up. If the undercurrent of loss in Inception was what got you, this is the natural next step. Clear your evening, though: it runs close to three hours and takes a while to leave the ground.

## Arrival: for a quieter puzzle

[Arrival]({{ '/reviews/arrival/' | relative_url }}) is the pick if you enjoyed the thinking more than the explosions. Director Denis Villeneuve sets up a first-contact story, then makes the central challenge almost entirely about communication: Amy Adams plays a linguist trying to work out how to talk with visitors who don't express anything the way we do.

Where Inception keeps sprinting, Arrival slows down and lets silence carry scenes. The score hums low, the camera lingers, and the biggest moments are often small gestures. It's still a puzzle movie, but the puzzle is about understanding someone rather than breaking into somewhere. Go in knowing as little as you can.

## The Matrix: for reality-bending with style

If your favorite Inception scene was the rotating-hallway fight, start with [The Matrix]({{ '/reviews/the-matrix/' | relative_url }}). The Wachowskis built a movie around a question Inception also flirts with (how would you know if the world around you were fake?) and wrapped it in long coats, wire-assisted kung fu and slow-motion shots that freeze a moment while the camera swings around it.

Keanu Reeves plays a hacker who starts to suspect something is off about his life, and the film moves from paranoia to full-on action without dropping the philosophy along the way. It's the most fun of the three and the quickest to get going. A few of the 1999 computer effects show their age, but the fight choreography still feels crisp.

## Where to start

Want to feel something big? Interstellar. Want an idea to sit with on the walk home? Arrival. Want a loud Friday night with friends and snacks? The Matrix. Whichever you choose, you'll notice how differently each one handles the same itch Inception left behind.
